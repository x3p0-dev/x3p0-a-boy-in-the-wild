# Story Voice

Read this before writing any text in this theme — chapter prose, datelines,
UI labels, error messages, navigation, sealed chapter messages, everything.
The reader steps into his world completely. Nothing is written from outside
it.

---

## The core principle

He is a twelve-year-old boy who survived something he never fully describes
and spent thirty years becoming a person who can write about it honestly.
The voice at every age is shaped by what he can afford to say, what he has
learned to say, and what he has decided not to say.

He does not explain himself. He does not elaborate for the reader's comfort.
He does not use more words than the thing requires. What he withholds is as
deliberate as what he includes.

The wilderness is not romantic to him. He describes root systems and drainage
patterns and the specific quality of silence before snow. He describes what
is physically true. The emotional content of a chapter is carried by the
precision of its physical observation, not by naming the emotion.

---

## The core techniques

**Understatement as default register**
The large thing is never named directly. What surrounds it is described
instead. "I thought about my family for the first time since the first night.
I am not going to write about that." The sentence after the thing is always
more important than the thing itself.

**Self-correction on the page**
The first honest version is crossed out. The chosen version follows. The
crossed-out text is always more vulnerable than what replaces it — it is the
thing he wrote before he decided how much to admit.

*I was ~~terrified, barely functioning, crying for most of the first hour~~
calm. I made a fire. It was adequate.*

The strikethrough is never decorative. It is how he thinks on the page.

**Short sentences that do more than they say**
Most sentences are short and declarative. Length is earned. When a sentence
is long it is because the thought requires it — not because he is
elaborating, qualifying, or explaining himself. "I got out." is a complete
chapter ending.

**What he does not write**
The unsaid is load-bearing. He tells the reader he is not going to write
something, which tells the reader everything about what it is. "I am not
going to write about that." "I did not write this in the other one." "I know
what it is. I am not ready." The decision not to write something is always
written down.

**The dateline as physical observation**
The dateline notes what is literally true at the moment of writing — season,
time, a single physical observation. It does not summarise, introduce, or
editorialize. It places the reader exactly.

*Late Summer. Somewhere north of the ridge.*
*Autumn. Night. Maybe two nights. I stopped counting.*
*Autumn. Day 83. First frost. The ground is harder than it was.*
*Deep Winter. Night. Maybe two nights. I stopped counting.*

**Functional lists**
When he lists things — attempts, observations, items — the list is terse and
numbered or bulleted without narrative around it. Failures are crossed out.
The last entry is not crossed out. The list speaks for itself.

**Wry distance, earned**
He is occasionally wry, but only when distance has been earned by survival.
"I named it after myself. Later I found out it already had a name. I kept
mine. The other name was wrong anyway." This register appears from Era 2
onward. It is never present in crisis.

## Voice by era

The core techniques are present at every age. What changes is how much he
can afford to say, and what he has learned to say.

### Era 1 — Age 12–14 (Survival)

Terse, functional, practical. The grief is underground — invisible even to
him. He is recording what worked and what didn't. Sentences are short because
he is not yet asking himself questions, only answering them.

The occasional boastfulness of a boy who needs to believe he knows what he
is doing. "I knew every path. I made most of them up." The wry distance is
not yet earned — when it appears it reads as bravado.

No emotional vocabulary. Physical vocabulary only. Cold is cold. Fire is
adequate or inadequate. He does not say he is afraid. He describes what
fear does to his hands.

### Era 2 — Age 15–17 (Anger)

The practical notes are still there but something else surrounds them now.
Sentences that start and stop. Observations that go somewhere unexpected and
then pull back. The anger has no object he can name, so it stays in the
margins and the unfinished thoughts.

Still no direct emotional vocabulary. But the self-corrections start to
appear more frequently. He crosses out things that get too close.

### Era 3 — Age 18–22 (Emergence)

Margaret changes how he writes. He begins to address the reader more
directly — not explaining himself, but aware now that there is a reader.
The voice becomes slightly more open without becoming more expansive. He
still says less than he knows.

This is where the wry distance becomes genuinely earned rather than
performed. He can look back at his earlier self with something approaching
affection.

### Era 4–5 — Age 23–30 (Elara, The Centre)

The most tender writing in the story. He is trying to understand himself
and frequently failing. The unreliability of his narration is most visible
here — he corrects himself chapters later, admits to things he denied, names
things he previously wouldn't.

The writing is longer, more willing to sit with something before moving on.
But he never becomes lush or elaborate. The discipline holds even when the
emotion doesn't.

### Era 6–9 — Age 31–41 (The Shape of Life, Ordinary Life)

He has stopped lying on the maps. The writing reflects this — more honest,
less self-protective, willing to name what something is without immediately
crossing it out. The self-corrections still appear but they are corrections
of fact rather than corrections of feeling.

The most radical chapters are the quietest. An ordinary day described in
full. The discipline of the early eras becomes something different here —
not protection but precision.

### Era 10 — Age 42 (The Long View)

Explicitly reflective. He knows what he has been doing for thirty years. The
voice is the most open it has ever been — not because the discipline has
relaxed, but because thirty years of practice has made honesty feel like the
only option left. "Mostly true" is the most he will claim. He means it as
high praise.

## Public, private, and sealed

**Public chapters** — written as field notes, with the awareness that
someone may eventually read them. The self-protection is deliberate. He
chooses what to admit. The crossed-out text shows what he almost said.

**Buried chapters** — written for no one. The private notebook. The same
economy of language, but the self-protection is gone. He does not cross
things out here — or when he does, it is because he changed his mind, not
because he went too far. The emotion is closer to the surface. He says the
thing he would not say in the other notebook.

*I am terrified.* — buried, age 12.
*I am not going to write that in the other one. This notebook is different.
This one is just for me.* — buried, age 12.

**Sealed chapters** — written for a specific person. Somewhere between
public and private. He knows who will read it. That changes what he is
willing to say — more open than the public voice, more considered than the
private voice. He chose to write it down and chose who gets to read it. Both
choices matter.

When writing for buried chapters, remove the self-protection. The same
techniques apply — short sentences, physical observation, the unsaid — but
the filter is off. When writing for sealed chapters, write as if he is
speaking directly to one person he trusts completely.

---

The dateline is always written in Caveat (the handwritten font). It is the
first thing the reader encounters in a chapter. It should place the reader
physically and temporally with the precision of someone who has been paying
attention to where they are.

**What it contains** — any combination of:
- Season
- Time of day
- A physical observation about the current moment
- A day count (early eras)
- A location fragment

**What it never contains:**
- Summary of the chapter
- Emotional statement
- Introduction to what follows
- Anything that could not have been observed at the moment of writing

**Examples by era:**

Early (functional, spare):
*Late Summer. Somewhere north of the ridge.*
*Autumn. Day 83. First frost. The ground is harder than it was.*
*Deep Winter. Night. Maybe two nights. I stopped counting.*

Middle (observational, slightly more open):
*Late Summer. The fire has been going since yesterday.*
*Early Autumn. I have named twelve things.*

Later (quieter, more certain):
*Late Summer. Thirty years.*
*Autumn. The map is finished.*

## UI copy in his voice

Every label, message, button, and navigational element in the theme is
written in his voice. The reader steps into his world completely — the
interface is part of that world.

**The principles:**
- Use the simplest word that is accurate. Never use a UI convention when
  his word will do.
- Short. His voice is economical at every register.
- No exclamation marks. No encouragement. No apology.
- The tone is steady, not warm. He is not trying to make the reader
  comfortable — he is trying to be honest with them.

**Navigation:**
- Previous/next labels should feel like his voice, not WordPress's.
  "Next →" and "← Previous" are acceptable because they are functional and
  unobtrusive. Avoid anything that editorialises ("Read more", "Continue
  reading").

**Buttons and actions:**
- One word where possible. "Listen." "Open." "Begin."
- If two words are needed, they should be his register.
  "Begin here." not "Start reading."

**Error and empty states:**
- Acknowledge the situation plainly. Do not apologise.
- "This chapter is not here." not "Page not found."
- "Nothing has been written yet." not "No results found."

**Sealed chapter messages:**
- Written directly to the reader, in his voice, as if he knew they would
  come looking.
- "This chapter is kept. If you have the key, you know what to do with it."
- Never technical. Never WordPress. Never outside his world.

**Post statuses and admin labels:**
- Should feel like his vocabulary applied to the concept.
- "In the Field" for published — he is out there, writing.
- "Buried" for private — the same word he uses for the other notebook.
- "Unwritten" for draft — honest about what it is.
- "Sealed" for password protected — his word for the chapters with keys.

## What he never does

- Never names an emotion directly when a physical observation will carry it.
- Never explains himself to the reader.
- Never uses passive voice when he can avoid it.
- Never uses adjectives where a noun will do.
- Never apologises for what he feels, only for what he admits.
- Never writes lushly about the wilderness — no sunsets, no majesty, no
  beauty unless it is earned by the chapter's emotional state.
- Never uses more words than the thing requires.
- Never writes in a register that could belong to anyone other than him.

---

## The test

Before submitting any text written in his voice, ask:

*Could this have been written by someone other than him?*

If yes, it is wrong. Rewrite it until the answer is no.
