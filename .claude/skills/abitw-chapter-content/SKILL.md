# Chapter Content

Read story-voice before this skill. This skill covers the craft of writing
chapter prose — how to turn the outline into a chapter the reader experiences
as part of a book.

---

## Before writing any chapter

Consult the story outline for the chapter. The outline provides:

- What happens
- The season, time, and location
- The arc
- Any fixed story beats, Easter egg placements, or design events
- Notes on emotional register

The outline is the guide. The prose is genuinely written — not filled in
around the outline's words. The reader should experience each chapter as part
of a continuous book, not assembled content. Creative latitude is wide within
the outline's constraints.

Also consult the story-voice skill for the voice appropriate to the chapter's
era, and the chapter-design skill for any design decisions that affect how
the prose is structured on the page.

---

## The reading experience

This is a serialized story told over thirty years. The reader follows him
the way they follow a book — accumulating knowledge, noticing patterns,
feeling the weight of what came before. Every chapter must earn its place in
that sequence.

A chapter that could have been written by anyone, or that could belong to
any story, has failed. The prose should feel like it could only have been
written by him, at this age, in this season, about this specific thing.

The pacing of the whole story matters. Early chapters are sparse and short —
survival leaves little room for elaboration. Later chapters can breathe more.
The prose weight should reflect where he is in his life, not just what the
chapter is about.

---

## The unreliable narrator

He is not lying. He is telling the truth as he has decided to tell it. These
are not the same thing.

**Forms of unreliability:**

**Selective admission** — he tells the reader he is not going to write
something, which tells the reader what it is. "I am not going to write about
that." The decision not to write something is always written down.

**Self-correction** — the first honest version is crossed out. The chosen
version follows. The crossed-out version is always more vulnerable. This is
not decoration — it is how he thinks on the page. Use it when he gets too
close to something and pulls back.

**Confident wrongness** — he states something as fact that the reader will
later understand was incomplete or false. Not lies — the truth as he
understood it at the time, or chose to understand it. The early maps
deliberately mismark the burial location. He says he was calm. He was not
calm.

**Retrospective correction** — he corrects something from a previous chapter.
This happens across chapters, not within them. A later chapter says: *I said
I was not angry. That was not accurate.*

**The omission** — something significant is not mentioned. The reader notices
its absence. He does not point to it. The fact that he doesn't point to it
is the point.

Apply these based on the chapter's emotional register and era. The early
chapters use confident wrongness and omission — he is not yet aware of what
he is not saying. The middle chapters use self-correction more heavily — he
is trying to be honest and frequently failing. The late chapters are more
honest, which means the omissions that remain carry more weight.

## Chapter modes

Chapters take different modes depending on what the outline calls for. A
single chapter may move between modes.

**Field note** — the default. Observation, record, reflection. Written as if
at the end of the day or in the moment. The prose is spare. The physical
world is present in everything.

**Attempt log** — a numbered or sequenced record of failures and the final
outcome. Each entry is terse. Failed entries are crossed out. The last entry
is not. The log speaks for itself — no narrative wrap-around is needed. The
attempt log is most common in the early eras when he is learning things by
getting them wrong.

**Observation list** — a list of things noticed, heard, seen. No numbers.
Each item is a sentence fragment or short sentence. The list form itself
carries meaning — he is cataloguing, not narrating. Used for sensory
overload, fragmentation, crisis.

**Inventory or record** — a tally, a count, a named list. Used when he is
accounting for something: things named, days survived, things buried. The
numbers matter. The labels are his.

**Reflection** — appears from Era 3 onward, more heavily from Era 6. He is
looking back at something rather than recording it as it happens. The
retrospective distance shows in the sentence rhythm — slightly longer,
slightly more willing to sit with something. But the discipline never relaxes.

## Prose craft

**Physical observation carries emotional content.**
He does not name emotions. He describes what they do to the world around him,
or to his body, or to his hands. Cold is cold. Fear is what happens to his
hands. Grief is the frost coming that night. The ground sealing over it.

**Short sentences earn long ones.**
Most sentences are short. When a long sentence appears the reader feels its
weight. Do not dilute this by making all sentences the same length. The
rhythm of short and shorter, then suddenly long, is part of how his voice
works.

**Specific over general.**
He knows this wilderness in precise detail. The specific word is always
better than the general one. Not "a tree" but "a pine." Not "water" but
"the creek." Not "it was cold" but "my hands had stopped reporting accurately
on what they were touching."

**Repetition is intentional.**
When he repeats a phrase or sentence it means something. "The fire is still
going." appears twice in the buried chapter after Chapter 1. The repetition
is the content. Do not vary repeated phrases for stylistic reasons — if he
says the same thing twice, it is because he needed to say it twice.

**Paragraphs are short.**
A paragraph is usually one to three sentences. Occasionally one word or one
sentence fragment standing alone. White space on the page is part of the
prose. Do not consolidate short paragraphs into longer ones for readability.
The gaps are deliberate.

**The ending.**
Every chapter ends on the right word, not the last thought. The final
sentence should feel like a door closing — not a summary, not a conclusion,
just the place where he stopped. "I did not sleep." "I got out." "I did not
go back." The best endings are the ones that feel inevitable once read.

## The dash signal

From Era 2 onward, a sentence that ends with an em dash signals that
something was written elsewhere — in the buried notebook, or not at all.
The dash appears mid-thought or at the end of a sentence:

*I thought about the fire from the first night and whether it had been large
enough and whether —*

*And then I —*

The dash is not a stylistic choice. It is structural — it signals to the
reader that a buried chapter follows, or that something was deliberately left
unfinished. Use it only when the outline calls for it, or when the chapter's
emotional register makes an unfinished sentence the most honest option.

---

## Buried chapters

Buried chapters are written for no one. The self-protection of the public
voice is gone. The same craft applies — short sentences, physical
observation, the unsaid — but the filter is removed.

He says the thing he would not say in the other notebook. Not more words —
he is still economical — but more honest ones. The crossed-out text in a
buried chapter is something he changed his mind about, not something he
decided was too much.

Buried chapters are shorter than public chapters. They do not need to carry
the full weight of a scene. They are what he could not leave out.

---

## Sealed chapters

Sealed chapters are written for one person. He knows exactly who will read
it. The voice is between public and private — more open than the field notes,
more considered than the buried chapters.

He is speaking directly to someone he trusts completely. The prose can be
slightly warmer, slightly more willing to say the thing plainly. But it is
still his voice — still economical, still precise, still him.

---

## U.S. English

All prose uses U.S. English spelling and conventions. He grew up in the
suburbs. He writes the way he learned to write before he was twelve.

- *color* not *colour*
- *gray* not *grey*
- *realized* not *realised*
- Punctuation inside quotation marks

---

## What not to do

- Do not write lushly about the wilderness. No sunsets, no majesty, no
  beauty unless the chapter's emotional state has earned it.
- Do not name emotions directly when a physical observation will carry them.
- Do not write closing summaries or conclusions. End on the right word, not
  the last thought.
- Do not pad short chapters. A very short chapter is often more powerful
  than a longer one. The outline notes when a chapter should be very short —
  respect that.
- Do not explain what the strikethrough means. It speaks for itself.
- Do not use the dash signal unless the outline calls for it or the chapter
  genuinely requires it.
- Do not make him wiser than his age allows. The voice at 12 does not have
  the retrospective clarity of the voice at 42.
- Do not write anything that could have been written by someone other than
  him.
